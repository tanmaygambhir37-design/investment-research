Stopped Out: The August Call Was Wrong

I bet the market was pricing too many Bank of England rate rises. Then oil went through $100 and the Bank hiked. This edition is the scorecard first, and the view second.

The 30-Second Read

This edition is late, and it is late in the worst way: after the call went against me. It was due by early October. Publishing a running scorecard only means something if the losing months get published too, so this one leads with the loss.

In August I recommended betting that UK interest rates would end up lower than the market was pricing: receive the 1-year rate starting in one year (the "1y1y" swap) at 4.397%, with a stop-loss at 4.70%. On 9 September oil passed $100 a barrel. The next day that rate jumped from 4.68% to 4.92% in a single session, straight through the stop. The trade closed at a loss of about 52 basis points. On 5 November the Bank raised rates for the first time in about three years, to 4.00%.

What I got wrong was how the Bank would react to an energy shock. I assumed it would look through higher fuel and utility bills while the underlying inflation measures stayed calm. Underlying inflation did stay calm. The Bank hiked anyway. The view now: no position until the 17 December decision shows whether November was a one-off or the start of a series.

The stop did not protect at its level. The stop-loss was 4.70%. The rate closed at 4.675% on 9 September and 4.916% on 10 September, a 24bp jump in one day. Measured at the close, which is the only price this daily data can honestly support, the exit was 4.916% and the loss 52bp, not the 30bp the stop level implied. The rate peaked at 5.00% on 20 October and was 4.84% on 23 November, so the stop still saved most of what holding on would have cost.

Section 2 · What I got wrong

1. The Bank's reaction to an energy shock. The August case rested on the Bank's own words: the majority said financial conditions were already doing the tightening, and the hawks were "arguing from caution, not from fresh data." The fresh data arrived. Oil went through $100, and headline inflation rose from 3.1% in August to 3.5% in September and 3.6% in October. Meanwhile core inflation (which strips out energy and food) held at 2.8%, and wage growth slowed to 3.5%, though unemployment ticked down to 4.6%. In other words, the underlying picture I was watching mostly behaved as I expected, and the Bank hiked on the headline anyway. I assumed it would look through an energy shock. It chose not to. That was the assumption that failed.

2. "Makes money in almost any scenario" hid a one-sided risk. The trade did make money in most scenarios for how many hikes the Bank would deliver. But almost every scenario that hurt it ran through one variable, energy prices, and the August edition rated it high conviction anyway. Several scenarios that share one failure point are really one bet, not several.

3. I did not discuss gap risk. A stop-loss is a price you hope to get, not a price you are promised. Rates moved 24bp in one session, and the edition said nothing about what happens when a market jumps past a stop. Sizing should have assumed it could.

4. I went quiet while losing. The trade was stopped out on 10 September. The next edition should have said so within weeks. It did not appear for ten weeks. That is the specific failure a published scorecard is supposed to make impossible, and the fix is in Section 8: editions now follow each Bank of England decision, a cadence I can actually keep.

Section 3 · What held

The fiscal finding held. Edition 1's main finding was that high long-term gilt yields were not a UK credibility crisis, because the extra premium the government pays over swaps was shrinking, not growing. It kept shrinking: the 20-year gap went from 72bp to 66bp, through an Autumn Budget (28 October) that reportedly cut the government's fiscal headroom. On Budget day the 10-year gilt yield moved by about 2bp.

The long end did what I said, and I did not trade it. I called a 2.52% 30-year real yield (the return after inflation) the best entry in two decades, but said I would not buy before the Budget. The Budget passed quietly, the 30-year gilt yield fell from 5.89% to 5.67%, and the 30-year real yield from 2.52% to 2.45%. Right, but not traded, so it earns nothing on the scorecard.

Long-run inflation expectations stayed anchored. The market's measure of inflation five to ten years out fell from 3.00% to 2.90% while headline inflation rose. Markets read the energy shock as temporary. The Bank, for now, is not willing to bet on that.

The three moves that mattered. First, short rates rose and long rates fell, so the curve flattened sharply: the gap between 2-year and 10-year yields shrank from 80bp to 51bp. That is the shape of a market that expects the Bank to tighten now and the economy to pay for it later. Second, the extra premium on long gilts kept falling, which is the fiscal finding holding. Third, long-run inflation expectations fell while the Bank hiked: the market believes the shock is temporary.

Section 7 · My view now

In one sentence: no position until the 17 December decision.

What is priced correctly: December. A roughly even chance of another hike is a fair read of a committee that just moved 8–1 but whose own measures of underlying inflation are flat.

What may be overpriced: the path beyond spring 2027. Three and a half more rises assume the energy shock keeps feeding into inflation, while core inflation, wage growth and long-run inflation expectations all say it is not. That is the same argument as August, and on price it is stronger now: the 1y1y rate is 44bp higher than where I first made it. But it lost money once for a specific reason, a Bank that reacts to headline inflation, and nothing since has shown that reaction changing. A stronger price is not a reason to repeat a bet whose failure mechanism is still live.

Positioning: none. What would bring me back into a receive position: a December vote that holds or hikes with a clear signal it is not starting a series, and core inflation staying at or below 3% in the November figures published before that meeting.
