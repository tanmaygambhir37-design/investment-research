Reading the UK Gilt Curve

What the bond market is pricing for the Bank of England, and why it matters.
Two curves answer two different questions. Gilts tell you what the market charges the sovereign. Only the SONIA OIS curve tells you what the market thinks the MPC will do.  ·  RECEIVE 1Y1Y SONIA OIS · 4.397%

The 30-Second Read

The Bank of England voted 6–3 to hold at 3.75% on 30 July. The market prices a full hike by December and two and a half by July 2027. I think that is too much.

Most UK rates commentary reads policy expectations off gilt yields. You cannot: a gilt is the expected policy path plus term premium plus a supply concession plus whatever the post-LDI pension complex is doing. At 20 years those non-policy components are worth 72bp. The instrument that isolates policy is the SONIA overnight index swap, and the Bank publishes its own fitted OIS curve daily, free. This project uses OIS for the policy path and gilts for what gilts actually price.

The priced hikes are self-defeating. The six who held said financial conditions had already tightened enough — the Governor named "the upward sloping yield curve" itself. The more the market prices hikes, the less the Bank needs to deliver. Three of those six are closer to cutting than hiking.

The 30-year is not a fiscal crisis. At 5.89% it is reported as a credibility event. But over twelve months the 20-year gilt–OIS spread narrowed, from 84bp to 72bp. The 2-year rose 60bp against the 30-year's 37bp. This was a front-end event that travelled down the curve.

The trade: receive 1y1y SONIA OIS at 4.397%. It profits in any world with fewer than three hikes. It fails if services CPI re-accelerates or 2027 pay settlements exceed 4% — and the next edition will say so if it does.

Key Findings

Market prices roughly 62bp of additional tightening over the next twelve months. A full 25bp hike by the December MPC and two and a half by July 2027. I think the realistic range is 0 to 25bp.

The SONIA OIS curve, not gilt yields, is the correct instrument for extracting Bank of England policy expectations. A gilt bundles the policy path with term premium, a DMO supply concession, and post-LDI market-structure effects — at 20 years, those non-policy components are worth 72bp. An OIS has no principal exchange and references an overnight rate that tracks Bank Rate almost mechanically.

Section 1 — How to read this market

Two curves, two questions

The single most common mistake in UK rates commentary is to read Bank Rate expectations off gilt yields. You cannot. A gilt yield contains at least four things: the average expected path of Bank Rate over the bond's life; a term premium for locking money up and bearing the risk that rates move; a supply and fiscal concession — the discount the market extracts to absorb DMO issuance and Bank of England quantitative tightening; and market-structure effects — collateral value, benchmark status, and what the liability-driven investment (LDI) complex is doing that week.

Only the first is policy expectations. The other three are noise if the policy path is your question, and they are large: at the 20-year point they are currently worth 72 basis points.

The instrument that isolates policy expectations is the overnight index swap. A sterling OIS exchanges a fixed rate for compounded SONIA over the swap's life. SONIA tracks Bank Rate almost mechanically — it fixed at 3.7320% on 30 July against a Bank Rate of 3.75%. Because an OIS involves no principal exchange and its cashflows reference an overnight rate, it carries negligible credit risk and, at short maturities, negligible term premium. The forward path of SONIA implied by the OIS curve is, to a very good approximation, the market's expected path of Bank Rate. The Bank of England fits and publishes its own OIS curve daily, free, in the same dataset as its gilt curves — it is the correct tool and it is under-used. Gilts then get put to the job they are good at: telling you what the market charges the sovereign, over and above the risk-free policy path.

The four terms you need

Yield curve. Yield plotted against maturity, using the Bank of England's fitted zero-coupon curves — cleaner than quoted bond yields, which are contaminated by coupon effects.

Duration. Approximate percentage price change for a 1% move in yield. A 30-year zero has roughly 30 years of duration; a 2-year has 2 — the same 10bp move costs fifteen times more at 30 years than at 2. Curve trades must be DV01-neutral — sized so both legs have equal sensitivity — or they are a directional bet wearing a disguise.

Term premium. The extra yield over expected policy for taking duration risk. Rather than estimate it with an affine term structure model — a black box that cannot be defended line by line — this report uses the gilt–OIS spread as its observable proxy. Both legs price the same expected policy path, so the path cancels; what remains is compensation for holding a gilt rather than receiving fixed in a swap.

Forward rate. The rate implied today for a future period. If the 1-year rate is 4.07% and the 2-year is 4.23%, the market implies a 1-year rate starting one year from now of about 4.40% — the 1y1y, the cleanest single expression of where the market thinks Bank Rate is heading, and the security this report recommends receiving.

Section 2 — How we got here

Four moves explain today's level.

March 2020 — COVID. Gilt yields collapsed as the Bank cut to 0.10% and restarted asset purchases, then violently reversed during the global dash for cash: the 10-year fell 46bp in the fortnight to 9 March 2020, then rose 65bp in the nine days that followed, with the 20-year up 85bp. The lesson that survives: in a liquidity event the long end moves most, and correlation between "safe" assets goes to one.

September–October 2022 — LDI. The most important episode in the modern gilt market. Between the 22 September mini-Budget and 12 October, the 20-year gilt yield rose 116bp and the 30-year 109bp. UK defined-benefit pension schemes had used leveraged interest rate swaps and repo to hedge liabilities, posting gilts as collateral; when yields rose, they faced collateral calls, meeting them meant selling gilts, and selling gilts pushed yields higher. The Bank intervened with time-limited long-dated purchases and the 30-year fell 133bp over the following three weeks. Two things changed permanently: LDI funds now hold materially more liquidity buffer and less leverage (structurally weaker natural demand for long-dated gilts), and the DMO skewed issuance away from the long end — long conventional gilts are just £22.4bn of £246.2bn in the revised 2026-27 remit, 9.1%.

2023–2025 — disinflation and quantitative tightening. Bank Rate peaked and began falling; the Bank ran down its gilt portfolio. Through 2025 the market consistently priced cuts: in mid-2025 the 12-month-ahead OIS forward sat 30–75bp below spot. As of 17 July 2026 the Bank still held £491bn of gilts for monetary policy purposes.

March 2026 — the energy shock. The conflict in the Middle East disrupted energy markets. At the end of February 2026 the market priced 30bp of cuts over twelve months; by the end of March it priced 53bp of hikes — an 83bp swing in a single month, and the regime the market has been in since. Everything in this report is a consequence of that flip.

The UK long end is expensive to the taxpayer because sterling rates repriced, not because the market has newly lost faith in the UK's fiscal position. The gilt-specific premium is elevated versus history — the 20-year spread is at the 74th percentile since 2015, a structural consequence of LDI retreat and QT — but it is not deteriorating right now.

Butterflies. The 5s10s30s fly is at −24bp, the 31st percentile since 2015: the 10-year is rich relative to its wings. 2s5s10s at −29bp tells a similar story — a mild argument against using the 10-year as the long leg of a steepener.

Regime. Classified on the standard bull/bear × steepen/flatten grid using rolling one-month changes, 2026 has been a bear market — yields rose in 55% of sessions — with no dominant curve regime; steepeners and flatteners split roughly evenly. In 2025, by contrast, bear steepeners accounted for 45% of sessions.

Inflation compensation, done properly

UK index-linked gilts reference RPI, not CPI. RPI has historically run about 0.9pp above CPI, but from February 2030 RPI is aligned to CPIH, which collapses that wedge to roughly zero. A breakeven spanning 2030 therefore blends two different indices. Applying the wedge only to the pre-reform portion of each horizon:

The headline "3.4% at 30 years" becomes 3.27% CPI-equivalent — still 127bp above target, but compensation is lowest at 10 years and rises with maturity, the profile of an inflation risk premium that grows with horizon uncertainty rather than a forecast of a permanent overshoot. The clinching evidence: 5y5y forward inflation compensation is 3.00%, down from 3.13% a year ago. Long-run expectations are falling during an energy shock. Whatever else is wrong with the UK, the inflation anchor is holding.

Real yields. The 10-year real yield is 1.88% and the 30-year 2.52%, the latter at the 99.5th percentile since 1997 — levels last seen before the financial crisis. For a pension fund or insurer with real liabilities this is the best entry point in two decades, and the strongest argument that long gilts will eventually find a bid. It is not an argument that they will find it this quarter.

Section 5 — My market view

What is priced correctly

The September meeting — six basis points, roughly a 6% chance of a move. The MPC has just said it wants to "allow time to observe further evidence," and the decisive data will not exist by 16 September. The 10-year in isolation — at 5.10% with a 47bp gilt–OIS spread, priced for a policy path I disagree with, but its gilt-specific premium is defensible. Long-run inflation — a 10-year CPI-equivalent breakeven of 2.91% is roughly fair for a central bank five years above target dealing with an energy shock. No edge, no trade.

What is overpriced — hikes. This is the core view.

The market prices +24bp by December and +62bp over twelve months. I think the realistic range is 0 to 25bp, central case one hike or none. Four reasons.

1. The reaction function is self-limiting. The majority's stated rationale for holding was that "the significant tightening of financial conditions that had occurred since the conflict started was providing sufficient insurance against the upside risks to inflation." Bailey named the yield curve itself as part of the tightening. When the market prices hikes, financial conditions tighten, and the MPC's need to hike falls — priced tightening substitutes for delivered tightening.

2. The vote is further from a hike than 6–3 suggests. Dhingra called a hike "disproportionate." Taylor sees the economy "drifting further toward deficient demand" and wants to resume cuts once uncertainty clears. Ramsden would "consider resuming the cutting cycle" if risks subside. That is three of the six who are closer to cutting than hiking.

3. The hawks are arguing from asymmetric loss functions, not from data. Greene: "there has been scant evidence of second-round effects, but I expect some to emerge." Mann's decisive input was the geopolitical development, not the inflation prints, which she concedes "have continued to moderate." Pill acknowledges staff found neither a money overhang nor expectation de-anchoring.

4. The evidence that would settle it does not arrive in time. The Committee said the key uncertainty is second-round effects in wage-setting, and that "indications of 2027 pay settlements were yet to come." The market prices a full hike by 17 December; the Committee expects to be starting to learn by then.

What is underpriced

The probability the next move is a cut. The OIS curve prices essentially zero chance of a cut over the next two years. Three MPC members have effectively said they would vote for one if the energy shock fades. The Bank's own milder published scenario assumes de-escalation and weaker household consumption — in that world Bank Rate is below 3.75% within a year, and that optionality is free at current levels. The long end, on valuation. A 30-year real yield of 2.52% is a two-decade high, and long conventional supply is just 9.1% of the FY26-27 remit. But the Autumn Budget is a scheduled, binary, unhedgeable event, and I am not paid to be early into one.

Section 6 — Cross-asset implications

Sterling. GBP/EUR is 1.1697, up 2.7% from 1.1386 pre-conflict — almost entirely a rate-differential story. If hikes get priced out, sterling gives that back; short GBP/EUR is a correlated way to hold the same risk. GBP/USD at 1.3457 is essentially unchanged.

UK banks. Higher-for-longer front-end rates support net interest margins directly, and the rolling hedge banks run to smooth deposit income reprices upward as older tranches mature into today's higher rates — a tailwind roughly locked in regardless of what the MPC does next. If hikes get priced out, the near-term NIM benefit fades at the margin but the hedge tailwind does not. The larger risk to UK banks is impairments if a "deficient demand" scenario plays out, not rates.

REITs and long-duration equity. The 30-year real yield at 2.52% is the discount rate that matters for real assets, at a two-decade high. This is a valuation headwind that does not resolve until long real yields fall, and the front end likely rallies first while the long end lags — REITs are not the way to express this view.

Sterling credit. All-in yields are attractive on a multi-decade view, but spread is the wrong risk to add here: if the adverse scenario hits (second-round effects force a hike into a weak economy), spreads widen and rates sell off together. Prefer the rate expression.

Equity valuations. A 10-year gilt at 5.10% is a hard number for the equity risk premium to compete with. If the front end rallies on hikes being priced out, that is mildly supportive for domestic equity through the growth channel, not the discount-rate channel.

Section 7 — What could prove me wrong

My thesis fails if second-round effects show up in the data. Concretely, I am wrong if:

  • Services CPI re-accelerates for two consecutive months — the Committee's primary gauge of domestic persistence and the most likely single trigger.
  • Private-sector regular pay growth turns back up. Taylor's "target-consistent rates" is load-bearing for the doves; if it reverses, the vote flips.
  • 2027 pay settlements print above 4% — the Committee's named decisive evidence, arriving late this year.

Inflation surprises. The Committee expects headline CPI to rise from 2.6% as energy passes through — anticipated. The danger is a print that overshoots even that, since attentiveness above a threshold is self-fulfilling. A 3.5%+ headline print would damage this view even with benign underlying detail.

Energy re-escalation. A sustained move in Brent above $100 or UK gas above 175p/therm would make the hawks' risk-management case decisively, and I would exit rather than argue.

Fiscal policy. My biggest exposure on the long-end call is the Autumn Budget forcing an upward NFR revision. Note this would not invalidate the front-end trade — fiscal tightening is disinflationary — but it would make "wait for the Budget" look like the right call for the wrong reason, and I should say so if it happens.

The strongest argument against my own thesis, on the record. My central claim is that priced hikes are self-defeating: the more the market prices, the less the Bank must deliver. But that cuts both ways — if hikes get priced out, financial conditions ease, and an MPC currently comfortable only because conditions are tight would face a genuine reason to move. Some hike premium may be permanently embedded in the front end precisely because the pricing is what stops it hiking. That is why my target on the 1y1y is 3.95% rather than 3.75%: the last 20bp of this trade is unlikely to be there, and anyone sizing it should assume they capture 40bp, not 65bp.

The structural risk I cannot hedge. The Bank still holds £491bn of gilts for monetary policy, and the natural long-end buyer base is permanently smaller post-LDI. If QT runoff meets a Budget that increases issuance while pension demand stays absent, the long end can cheapen a long way beyond what any valuation screen says is fair. In 2022 the 20-year moved 116bp in three weeks. Valuation is not a catalyst, and a cheap asset can get much cheaper.

Section 8 — Three questions a client would ask today

"The market prices two and a half hikes and you say fade it. If you're right, why hasn't the market figured this out?" Because the market is not forecasting, it is buying insurance. Energy risk is skewed to the upside and the distribution is fat-tailed, so the risk-neutral path sits above the modal path. The trade does not need the market to be stupid; it needs the modal outcome to happen — and the payoff structure tells you the insurance is priced high: it still makes money if two full hikes are delivered. The honest risk: if the insurance is correctly priced and the tail materialises, I lose. That is why the position is sized as a directional receiver with a stop, not a leveraged carry trade.

"The 30-year is at a 28-year high. Is the UK in a fiscal crisis, and should I be worried about my long-dated holdings?" No, and the data is unambiguous. Over the past year the 30-year gilt yield rose 37bp while the 20-year gilt–OIS spread narrowed from 84bp to 72bp. If this were a fiscal credibility event, that spread would be widening — it is not. What you should worry about is more structural: the natural buyer of long-dated gilts is permanently smaller post-2022, and the Bank is still running off £491bn. A 2.52% 30-year real yield is the best entry in two decades; my only reason for not buying today is the Autumn Budget.

"I have to put money to work this quarter. Where's the best risk-adjusted place on the sterling curve?" The front end, receiving. A 1-year OIS at 4.07% against SONIA at 3.73% pays 33bp a year to hold a position that also expresses my highest-conviction view. For more upside, receive 1y1y at 4.40%, which makes money in every scenario up to two delivered hikes. Not this quarter: the 30-year, until the Budget clears; sterling credit; and breakevens, where I see no edge at all.

Methodology

Extract the policy path from BoE OIS instantaneous forwards, interpolated onto scheduled MPC dates. Decompose the gilt curve as gilt zero-coupon yield less OIS zero-coupon yield by tenor — the shared policy path cancels, leaving term premium, fiscal and supply concession, and market-structure effects. Measure slope and curvature (2s5s, 2s10s, 5s10s, 10s30s, 5s30s and butterflies) in levels and rolling percentiles, classified on the bull/bear × steepen/flatten grid. Convert RPI breakevens to CPI-equivalent by applying the ~0.9pp wedge only to the pre-February-2030 portion of each horizon. Measure event studies (COVID, LDI, the 2026 energy shock) in bp by tenor over defined windows. This project deliberately does not implement an affine term structure model: the gilt–OIS spread is coarser, but every basis point of it can be explained under questioning, which matters more than theoretical elegance for how this work is used.
