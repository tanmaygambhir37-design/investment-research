Pre-Registered IPO Call · Comparable-Multiples Valuation · 23 September 2026

Oura Inc. (Nasdaq: OURA, proposed)

Can a model that knows nothing about hardware price a hardware company's IPO?  ·  CALL · $12.95bn · $40.36/sh  AWAITING PRICING
The 30-Second Read

Oura makes the Oura Ring and is on its Nasdaq roadshow at $40 to $44 a share, which values the company at $12.8bn to $14.1bn. Before it prices, I ran its filing numbers through my own simulator, VC Playbook, with the exact settings I used on Bending Spoons in July and nothing re-tuned. $1.62bn of annualised revenue at 8x gives $12.95bn, or $40.36 a share: near the bottom of the range. The catch is that 80% of Oura's revenue is hardware sold at a 55% gross margin, and 8x is a software multiple. So this tests two things at once: whether a simple, untuned model can price an IPO before the market does, and whether the market will pay software prices for a ring. The error gets published either way.

Why the IPO price and not the day-one close: the IPO price is what institutional investors agree the company is worth after a week of meetings, which is the question the model answers. The first day is driven by a small float (about 50m of 320.9m shares trade) and by banks pricing a little low on purpose. Bending Spoons priced at $18.4bn and closed its first day near $25bn; the "within 4%" result there was measured against the $18.4bn.

My read on where it prices (my own probabilities, not a model output): inside the range 55%, above it 30%, below it 15%. The range was set below the $16bn+ figure reported before launch7, which suggests the bankers want the book to clear comfortably; above-range is a real possibility, as Bending Spoons showed; below-range is least likely for a company that is profitable and still growing 74%.

The Business

Oura sells a health-tracking ring plus a paid monthly membership that unlocks its sleep, readiness and activity insights. It was founded in Finland in 20131 and is run by CEO Tom Hale8. At 30 June 2026 it had 5.0m paid members, double the 2.5m a year earlier, with about 85% 12-month retention and a daily-to-monthly active user ratio of about 65%1. It guides to about 5.7m paid members by the end of its fiscal year on 30 September2.

The clearest piece of moat is legal, not technical. In September 2025 the US International Trade Commission found that Ultrahuman and RingConn infringed an Oura patent on how the ring is built. Ultrahuman's rings were barred from US import and sale; RingConn settled and now pays Oura royalties under a licence6. The bigger competitive question is Apple, Samsung and Google, all of which have added sleep and health features that overlap with Oura's8.

Three things the table says that the headline growth rate doesn't. First, gross margin fell 13 points in FY2025, from 64.9% to 51.9%, in the year revenue more than doubled, and has only recovered to 55%. The filing figures don't tell me the cause, so I don't assign one. Second, adjusted EBITDA grew 28% while revenue grew 74%, so the adjusted margin fell from 12.0% to 8.8%; sales and marketing grew 84%, faster than revenue. Third, net income jumped from $1.6m to $60.8m in the same period that the adjusted EBITDA margin fell. Those two can both be true only if the change sits below operating profit or inside the adjustments. I have not reconciled that line by line, so I treat the net-income jump as Medium confidence as evidence of durable profitability, even though the number itself is High confidence.

A separate item that looks alarming and isn't: the filing shows a $924.3m net loss attributable to common stockholders for the nine months, driven by a $985.0m "deemed dividend" to preferred stockholders13. That is an accounting entry tied to the pre-IPO preferred share structure, not an operating loss.

Hardware or Subscription? The Question the Price Is Really Asking

Over the nine months, hardware brought in $974.0m and membership $240.5m: 80% / 20%1. Annualised, membership is about $321m a year. Divided by members, that is roughly $64 to $86 per member per year, depending on whether you use the June 2026 count (5.0m) or a simple average of the June 2025 and June 2026 counts (3.75m). That range is my arithmetic and Low confidence; Oura does not disclose average revenue per member.

The bankers' range implies 7.9x to 8.7x annualised revenue. That is a multiple usually paid for subscription businesses, applied to a company whose revenue is four-fifths hardware at a 55% gross margin. Either the membership layer is worth far more than its 20% share of revenue suggests, or the range is generous.

This is the same question as my Technogym DCF: should a hardware maker get software multiples? There, isolating the software business on its own margins changed the answer. Here I can't do that, because Oura does not disclose gross margin separately for hardware and membership (Not Disclosed). The honest version of this page says so rather than estimating a split into existence. Once Oura reports as a public company, it becomes the first clean public comparable for "hardware plus membership", which is the open question on my Ideas page about Garmin and Peloton.

Put together: the company raises almost no net cash for itself, most of the shares come from existing investors, and the range came in below the reported target and only 1.2x to 1.3x the price paid eleven months earlier. None of that is a red flag on its own. It does mean this IPO is mostly a liquidity event, and it explains why the bankers may have set the range to clear rather than to stretch.

What Would Change My Mind

These are about the company over the next twelve months, separate from the pricing call.

More bullish if: paid members reach the 5.7m guided for 30 September; membership rises above 20% of revenue; gross margin keeps recovering toward the FY2024 level of 65%; the adjusted EBITDA margin stops falling.

More bearish if: the member guide is missed; gross margin stalls at about 55% while sales and marketing keeps outgrowing revenue; a large competitor ring (Samsung's is the obvious one) gains traction in the US despite the patent position; the first public quarters show growth decelerating faster than the 123% to 74% step already visible.
